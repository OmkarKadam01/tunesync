01Problem & vision

A stadium already contains fifty thousand independently addressable light sources. Nobody is driving them.

Every phone in an arena has a high-output LED, a bright screen, a haptic motor and a microphone. Collectively they are a lighting rig with more pixels than anything hanging from the truss — but they sit idle, or worse, they all record video. The three existing ways to light a crowd all have a cost: LED wristbands run $2–6 a head and end up in landfill, the truss rig only points down at the crowd rather than out from it, and "everyone turn your torch on" produces a static field with no rhythm.

TuneSync is an Android app that makes each phone an autonomous lighting fixture that knows the song. It does this without a server, without Wi-Fi, and without the venue's cooperation — the intelligence lives on the handset.

1.1The wedge

Phase 1 proves the hard part in a bedroom, not a stadium: import a song from the device, strip the vocals out of the analysis, find the beats, show the user the map, and flash the torch on the strongest hits in perfect time with local playback. If that isn't convincing on a kitchen table it will never be convincing in an arena, and it needs no venue, no contract and no crowd to test.

02What CUE actually does

You pointed at CUE as the reference implementation. Their mechanism is not the one you described, and the difference is the whole strategy.

CUE does not recognise the song. There is no fingerprinting, no ACR, no beat detection on the handset. Instead the production team injects an inaudible carrier into the venue PA alongside the music, and every phone in the building decodes it. From their own published material and SDK documentation:

Carrier band
17.5–19.5kHz near-ultrasonic, above most adult hearing but inside every phone mic's range
Payload rate
~25 bpshigh-reliability — enough for a cue index and a frame counter, not a light show
Sync accuracy
±125 ms"to the nearest eighth of a second", their words
Proven scale
80,000attendees, 150 m propagation, indoor and outdoor

The show itself is pre-loaded; the audio channel only carries "start now, you are at frame N." They have since moved the client from a native app to a plain web page reached by QR code, using the browser's camera-torch API — which removed the install step entirely. Production integration goes further than audio: they expose UDP triggers so the crowd show can be cued from the same desk that runs the video wall and house lights.

The strategic read

CUE's moat is venue relationships and a box in the PA rack, not signal processing. Their approach is more reliable than anything we can do from the audience side — and completely unavailable to an artist who hasn't signed a deal with them, or to a fan who just wants their phone to do something at a gig.

Fingerprint alignment is the thing they structurally cannot offer: it works uninvited. That is our wedge. We then add an ultrasonic tier for the venues that do sign, because for a 50,000-seat show it is simply the better mechanism and we should not pretend otherwise.

Two lessons we should copy outright. First, the show data must be on the device before the moment — whatever the sync channel, it carries a clock, never a light show. Second, the install step is the biggest funnel loss in the entire product. Asking a fan in row 300 to find, download and open a 40 MB APK during a 90-second stadium moment will convert in the low single digits. See §23 for how we plan around that.

4.1Permanent non-goals

  • Playing DRM-protected streams. Spotify, Apple Music and YouTube Music files are not readable by third-party apps and never will be. We import files the user owns.
  • Identifying a song we have never seen. That is an ACR service and a licensing relationship. We match against tracks the user has already imported.
  • Being a music player. Phase 1 playback exists to rehearse the show, not to replace the user's player.
  • Exceeding the flash safety ceiling for artistic reasons. Not negotiable at any tier. See §15.

05Import & decode

Getting audio into the app without asking for a permission we don't need.

5.1Use the document picker, not the media permission

The obvious route is READ_MEDIA_AUDIO plus a MediaStore query. Don't. ACTION_OPEN_DOCUMENT through the Storage Access Framework returns a persistable URI, reaches files MediaStore never indexed (Downloads, SD card, Drive), and requires no runtime permission at all. One fewer scary dialog on first run is worth more than a prettier in-app browser.

Take a persistable read grant immediately (takePersistableUriPermission) so the track survives a reboot, and copy the decoded analysis artefacts into app-private storage so a deleted or moved source file doesn't destroy the show.

  • IMP-01
    Import via SAF. Single and multi-select. Accept audio/* plus explicit MIME hints for MP3, AAC/M4A, FLAC, WAV, OGG/Vorbis, Opus.
  • IMP-02
    Persist the grant. Store the URI with a persistable read permission; re-verify on app start and surface a re-link prompt if revoked.
  • IMP-03
    Copy, don't reference. Analysis output, waveform peaks and fingerprints are written to app-private storage keyed by a content hash of the source, so the beat map outlives the file.
  • IMP-04
    Deduplicate by content. SHA-256 of the first and last 1 MB plus file length. Re-importing the same track reuses the existing map instead of re-analysing.
  • IMP-05
    Read tags for display only. Title, artist, artwork via MediaMetadataRetriever. Never trust tag duration — measure it from the decoder.

5.2Decode to analysis PCM

MediaExtractor → MediaCodec in asynchronous mode → 16-bit PCM. Then, for the analysis path only: downmix to mono by channel average, and resample to 22,050 Hz. Beat tracking gains nothing above ~11 kHz of content and the halved sample rate roughly halves every FFT downstream. Playback uses the original file untouched.

Found in build — the chain has its own group delay

Two systematic timing errors live in the analysis chain itself, and together they put every beat 57 ms early before either was fixed — most of the accuracy budget, spent on nothing.

First, STFT frames must be centred: timestamping a frame from its window start reports each onset half a window (46 ms) early. Second, spectral flux is a backward difference, so it peaks while magnitude is still rising, before the transient reaches the window centre. What remains is a fixed group delay measured at 0.60 hops using isolated impulses — not fitted to music, so it cannot encode a quirk of the fixtures. With both corrected, mean absolute beat error on clean material is 6–8 ms. ChainLatencyTest fails if a change to the window, hop or flux definition moves it.

Detail that will bite you

MP3 and AAC decoders emit encoder delay and padding. If you ignore it, every beat map is offset by 20–70 ms against the file — consistently wrong, and consistently blamed on the beat tracker. Read MediaFormat.KEY_ENCODER_DELAY and KEY_ENCODER_PADDING where present and trim the decoded stream before analysis. Where absent (older devices, some containers), fall back to the format's nominal delay and expose the global offset nudge in the editor (§8.3).

06Beat analysis pipeline

Six stages, all on-device, no model file, no network. "Ignoring the vocals" falls out of the second stage for free.

You framed this as "ignore vocals and map the beats." The clean way to get there is not vocal removal — it is harmonic–percussive source separation. Vocals, guitars, pads and bass are harmonic: they hold pitch, so they draw horizontal lines across a spectrogram. Drums are percussive: broadband transients, so they draw vertical lines. Median-filtering the magnitude spectrogram along each axis pulls the two apart, and the percussive residue is exactly the signal a beat tracker wants. It costs no model file, no download and a couple of seconds of CPU — where shipping a Demucs or Spleeter model would cost 30–80 MB of APK and up to three minutes of thermally-expensive inference per track for a map that is barely better.

6.1Stage parameters

StageMethodParameters and notes
STFTHann window, magnitude spectrogramn_fft 2048, hop 512 → 23.2 ms frames, 43 fps at 22,050 Hz. Fine enough to place a beat inside our ±20 ms budget after parabolic peak interpolation.
HPSSMedian filtering, soft (Wiener) maskHorizontal kernel ~0.2 s (17 frames) for harmonic; vertical kernel ~500 Hz (23 bins) for percussive. Soft mask with power 2 rather than a binary mask — binary masking produces artefacts that read as spurious onsets.
OnsetHalf-wave rectified log-magnitude fluxComputed on the percussive spectrogram, in three bands: low <200 Hz (kick), mid 200–2k (snare/clap), high >2k (hats). Keeping them separate is what later lets a show say "flash on kicks only".
TempoAutocorrelation of the onset envelopeLog-normal prior centred at 120 BPM, σ = 1.0 octave. Without the prior, half-time and double-time errors are the single most common failure — a 140 BPM track reported as 70 produces a show that flashes on every other beat and looks broken.
BeatsEllis dynamic-programming beat trackerMaximises cumulative onset strength subject to a tempo-consistency penalty. Global optimum over the whole track, so a weak bridge doesn't derail the grid.
DownbeatsLow-band periodicity + metre priorTest 3/4 and 4/4 hypotheses over bar-length windows of low-band energy. Emit a confidence; below 0.6 the show falls back to beat-level cues and the editor flags the track.
SectionsSelf-similarity novelty curveMFCC + RMS self-similarity matrix, checkerboard kernel. Gives verse/chorus/drop boundaries so intensity can rise into a chorus. P1.5

6.2Salience — the "highest nodes"

You described flashing on the peaks of the graph. That needs a defined number per beat, not an eyeball. Every beat b gets a salience in [0,1]:

// per beat, all terms normalised to the track's own distribution
salience(b) = 0.45 · flux_low(b)      // kick weight — the thing you feel
            + 0.25 · flux_total(b)    // overall transient energy
            + 0.20 · metric(b)        // 1.0 downbeat, 0.6 backbeat, 0.3 other
            + 0.10 · rms_local(b)     // section loudness, 2-bar window

Show generation then thresholds on salience rather than firing on every beat, which is both the aesthetic win and, at high tempo, the safety mechanism (§15.2). The weights are tunable per show style and exposed in the editor as a single "sensitivity" slider that moves the threshold, not the weights.

6.3Confidence, and admitting failure

The analyser must return a per-track confidence and be willing to say it failed. Rubato piano, free-time intros, ambient, spoken word and heavily swung jazz will not produce a usable grid, and a bad grid that pretends to be good is worse than an honest refusal. Confidence combines beat-interval variance, onset-envelope periodicity strength and the margin between the top two tempo hypotheses. Below 0.5 the app says so plainly and offers manual tap-tempo instead of shipping a broken map.

07Beat map format

One artefact, versioned, portable, and the contract between every subsystem downstream.

The beat map is the only thing Phase 1 produces and the only thing Phases 2 and 3 consume. Get the format right now. JSON is the interchange and debug form; the runtime loads a Protobuf encoding of the identical schema for parse speed and size.

{
  "schema": 2,
  "trackId": "sha256:9f2c…",          // content hash, not filename
  "durationMs": 243180,
  "sampleRate": 44100,              // of the SOURCE, for offset math
  "analysis": {
    "engineVersion": "1.4.0",        // re-analyse when this changes
    "confidence": 0.87,
    "tempoBpm": 128.02,
    "tempoStable": true,
    "meter": [4, 4],
    "meterConfidence": 0.91,
    "encoderDelayMs": 26.1            // already applied; recorded for audit
  },
  "beats": [                            // ~500 for a 4-min track
    { "tMs": 1874, "idx": 0, "bar": 1, "pos": 1,
      "salience": 0.92, "bands": [0.95, 0.41, 0.22] },
    …
  ],
  "sections": [
    { "startMs": 0, "label": "intro", "energy": 0.31 },
    { "startMs": 30020, "label": "chorus", "energy": 0.88 }
  ],
  "peaks": "…base64 u8[2048]…",        // waveform for the editor, precomputed
  "edits": {                            // user overrides, never overwritten by re-analysis
    "globalOffsetMs": -12,
    "muted": [143, 144],
    "added": [{ "tMs": 98450, "salience": 1.0 }]
  }
}
  • FMT-01
    Edits are a separate layer. Re-running analysis with a newer engine must preserve user edits. Store them as a diff against beat indices and timestamps so they can be re-anchored if indices shift.
  • FMT-02
    Version the engine, not just the schema. A map produced by engine 1.3 is silently re-analysed in the background when 1.4 ships; the user is told only if the result differs materially.
  • FMT-03
    Portable by design. No Android types, no absolute paths, no device-specific fields. A map produced on a Pixel must run unmodified on an iPhone in a later port and on the operator console.
  • FMT-04
    Bounded size. Target < 80 KB JSON / < 25 KB Protobuf per track so a 30-track set list fits in a single distributable bundle.

08The visualisation

The screen you sketched: waveform, beats on top, and the flash train underneath so the mapping is visible rather than asserted.

This screen is the product in Phase 1. It is where the user decides whether to trust the analysis, and it must show three synchronised lanes over the same timebase:

  1. Waveform. The symmetric peak-bar rendering from your reference — min/max envelope per column, drawn from the precomputed peaks array so scrolling never touches the audio file.
  2. Beat lane. A tick per beat, height proportional to salience, downbeats taller and accented. This is the "graph" — it makes an unstable grid visible at a glance, because wobble in the tick spacing is far easier to see than to hear.
  3. Cue lane. What will actually happen: torch pulses as filled blocks with real width (the pulse duration), screen colour as a tinted band, haptics as small marks. Above-threshold beats show a cue; below-threshold beats show only a faint tick, so the effect of the sensitivity slider is immediately legible.

8.1Rendering requirements

  • VIS-01
    60 fps while scrubbing on a mid-range device for a 10-minute track. Draw on a Canvas in a Compose drawWithCache, not with composables per beat.
  • VIS-02
    Pinch to zoom from whole-track to one-bar. At bar zoom, show the millisecond grid so a user can nudge a beat precisely.
  • VIS-03
    The playhead is driven by AudioTrack.getTimestamp(), not by a timer. See §13.4 — this is the difference between a playhead that looks right and one that looks right.
  • VIS-04
    Live torch mirror. A dot in the corner lights when the torch fires, so the user can verify sync while looking at the screen rather than at the back of the phone.
  • VIS-05
    Colour-blind safe. Beat and cue lanes must be distinguishable by shape and position, not hue alone.

8.2Progressive disclosure during analysis

Analysis takes seconds, and a spinner for eight seconds feels broken. Stream the stages: the waveform can be drawn as soon as decode passes each chunk, the tempo estimate lands about halfway, and beats fill in from the front. The user watches the map build. This also means the cancel button has something meaningful to cancel.

09Show generation

Turning a beat map into a timeline of hardware events, with the safety limiter as the last gate before anything can fire.

A show is a beat map plus a style, compiled into a flat, sorted list of cues. Compilation happens once, ahead of time. The runtime never makes a creative decision at 3 ms notice — it only executes a pre-sorted list, which is what makes the timing budget in §14 achievable.

ChannelCue payloadNotes
Torch{tMs, durMs, strength}Duration 40–120 ms typical. strength maps to turnOnTorchWithStrengthLevel on Android 13+, ignored below (binary on/off).
Screen{tMs, durMs, rgb, curve}Curve is step, decay or ramp. Rendered at display refresh, so it can be smooth where the torch cannot.
Haptic{tMs, amplitude}VibrationEffect.createOneShot, or a composition primitive on devices that expose one. Off by default.

9.1Built-in styles

StyleRuleFits
Pulse defaultTorch on every beat with salience > 0.55; 60 ms; strength scaled by salienceAlmost everything. The safe default.
AnchorDownbeats only, 100 ms, full strength, screen holds a colour across the barSlow songs, anthems, high-tempo tracks where every beat would breach the flash cap
KickFires on low-band flux above threshold, ignoring the beat grid entirelyElectronic and hip-hop, where the kick pattern is the hook and isn't always on the beat
SwellNo torch. Screen brightness follows the smoothed onset envelopeBallads, the reduced-flash accessibility mode, and any user who opts out of strobing
SectionsScreen colour changes per section, torch density scales with section energyFull-song shows where a single look for four minutes gets boring

9.2The compile step

  1. Select beats by style rule and salience threshold.
  2. Apply edits — mutes removed, additions merged, global offset applied.
  3. Apply per-section intensity multipliers.
  4. Subtract the device's measured torch latency (§13.1) so scheduled time means photons out, not call made.
  5. Run the safety limiter (§15.2). Non-optional, non-bypassable, and it may delete cues.
  6. Merge overlapping cues, sort by time, freeze to an immutable array.

Compilation is deterministic: same map, same style, same device profile → byte-identical cue list. That property is what makes the show testable.

10Live sync — fingerprint alignment

Phase 2's consumer path. The phone hears the PA, works out where in the song it is, and runs a show it already has.

This is not song identification. We already know which song it is — the user imported it. The only unknown is position: which millisecond of the track is playing right now, and how fast the phone's clock is drifting against the venue's. That is a much easier problem than Shazam solves, and it can be solved entirely on-device against a local index of a few dozen tracks.

10.1Requirements

  • LIV-01
    Cold lock in ≤ 3 s at 75 dB SPL with typical crowd noise, p90. The user should tap "listen" and see it catch before they lose faith.
  • LIV-02
    Warm re-lock every 500 ms, constrained to ±2 s around the predicted position, costing < 6% of one core.
  • LIV-03
    Unprocessed capture. Request MediaRecorder.AudioSource.UNPROCESSED where PROPERTY_SUPPORT_AUDIO_SOURCE_UNPROCESSED is true; else VOICE_RECOGNITION; never MIC with AGC and noise suppression mangling the spectrum. Capture at 44.1 or 48 kHz through AAudio in low-latency mode.
  • LIV-04
    Track drift, don't just re-seek. A Kalman filter over (offset, rate) absorbs crystal drift between the venue's playback clock and the phone's — typically 10–50 ppm, so 0.6–3 ms per minute. Small, but it accumulates across a set and jumping the show to correct it looks worse than the drift.
  • LIV-05
    Coast, don't stop. If confidence drops, keep running on the filter's prediction for up to 8 s before declaring loss of lock. A brief crowd roar over the chorus must not kill the show.
  • LIV-06
    Show the state honestly. Three states only — Listening Locked Coasting — with a confidence bar. Never fake a lock.

10.2Where this fails, honestly

Fingerprint alignment against a studio master fails on the thing artists most want to light: the live performance. A band playing the song on stage is a different recording — different tempo, different arrangement, different everything. The fingerprints will not match. Phase 2 works for walk-out music, DJ sets, arena playlists, pre-recorded backing tracks and stadium moments where a known cut is played over the PA. It does not work for a live band, and we should say so in the marketing rather than have a tour manager discover it in soundcheck. Live performance needs the ultrasonic tier, or a click-track feed, and that is a real reason §12 exists.

11The 3 ms/metre problem

The most important physical constraint in the product, and the one most likely to be discovered late.

Sound travels at roughly 343 m/s. Every phone syncing to what its microphone hears is therefore syncing to a version of the song delayed by its distance from the speakers — about 3 ms per metre. In a 100-metre bowl, the back row is nearly a third of a second behind the front row, and no amount of DSP fixes it, because the information genuinely has not arrived yet.

11.1What to do about it

OptionMechanismVerdict
Accept it v1Do nothing. Each phone syncs to what it hears.Ship this. The result is a flash wave rolling away from the stage at the speed of sound — which is what a real crowd wave looks like and reads as intentional. It is also exactly what CUE's ultrasonic system produces, for the same reason.
Seat-section inputUser picks their section; app subtracts a per-section delay from a venue map.Adds a friction step at the worst moment and needs a venue database. Only worth it for a pro tier where the operator wants a flat front.
Network time baseNTP-disciplined clock plus a show start timestamp; audio only used to confirm.Gives a genuinely flat wavefront, but requires connectivity in a building where 50,000 people are all on the same cell. Pro tier only, with the audio path as fallback.
Deliberate waveAdd delay on purpose, per section, to sweep light across the bowl.A genuinely good feature for Phase 3 — if you can measure position you can choreograph it. Park it.
Design consequence

Because the spread is unavoidable, tight per-phone timing still matters enormously. The wave only reads as a wave if each phone is crisp against its own local audio. Sloppy 80 ms jitter on top of the propagation gradient turns a wave into mush. This is why the ±20 ms budget in §14 is worth fighting for even though the room has 233 ms of spread in it.

12Ultrasonic venue tier

Phase 3. CUE's mechanism, offered to venues who want a guarantee rather than a best effort.

12.1Signal design

ParameterValueReasoning
Band17.6 – 19.4 kHzAbove the hearing of most adults, below the point where phone mics and PA drivers roll off hard. Matches the band CUE proved at stadium scale.
Modulation8-tone MFSK3 bits/symbol. Robust against the multipath and reverb that destroy phase-coherent schemes in a concrete bowl.
Symbol rate~20 Bd50 ms symbols. Long enough that a reflection arriving 30 ms late still lands inside the symbol.
Payload rate~25 bps netAfter a Reed–Solomon outer code and preamble overhead. Same order as CUE, for the same physical reasons.
Packetpreamble + 24 bitsChirp preamble for timing, then 8-bit show ID + 14-bit frame counter + 2-bit command. Repeats continuously — a phone joining late locks on the next packet.
Frame resolution125 msThe counter names an eighth-second slot; sub-frame position comes from preamble correlation, which is accurate to a few ms.

12.2What will go wrong with it

  • The PA eats the carrier. House systems are routinely low-passed at 16–18 kHz, and limiters and lossy codecs in the distribution chain will attack a constant high-frequency tone. Requires a soundcheck procedure and a measured headroom report per venue. This is an install service, not a download.
  • Teenagers can hear it. Hearing at 17.6 kHz is common under 25 — exactly the audience at most of these events. Sweep upward within the band and keep level as low as the error rate allows.
  • Phone mics are hostile to it. Many devices apply anti-aliasing roll-off, and every noise-suppression path treats a steady tone as noise to remove. UNPROCESSED capture is mandatory here, not preferred, and devices that don't offer it fall back to the fingerprint tier.
  • Bandwidth is tiny. 25 bps carries a clock and nothing else. Show content must be distributed before the event, which reinforces the §7 requirement that beat maps be small and portable.

13Output & scheduler

Where the software meets the hardware, and where all the timing is won or lost.

13.1Torch driver

Torch control is CameraManager.setTorchMode(). Notably it needs no CAMERA permission, because no camera device is opened — a genuinely useful fact for onboarding, and worth confirming per OEM. On Android 13+, turnOnTorchWithStrengthLevel() gives graded brightness, gated on FLASH_INFO_STRENGTH_MAXIMUM_LEVEL > 1.

  • TCH-01
    Capability probe on first run. Measure actual round-trip latency and the fastest reliable toggle rate, store a device profile, and never re-measure unless the OS version changes. Probe runs with the screen dimmed and asks the user to point the phone at a wall for two seconds.
  • TCH-02
    Subtract measured latency at compile time. The scheduler fires L milliseconds early where L is the device's measured actuation delay, typically 5–40 ms and occasionally worse.
  • TCH-03
    Graded strength where available. Map salience to strength on Android 13+; fall back to modulating pulse duration below 13, since a longer flash reads as brighter.
  • TCH-04
    Register a torch callback. registerTorchCallback catches another app or the quick-settings tile stealing the torch, so we can pause rather than fight it.
  • TCH-05
    Duty-cycle guard. Hard cap the torch at 35% duty over any rolling 10 s window. Beyond that, LED drivers current-limit or cut out entirely, and the phone gets hot enough that the user notices.

13.2Screen

The screen is the better instrument and should not be treated as the torch's sidekick. It does colour, it does smooth ramps at display refresh rate, it costs nothing thermally compared to the LED, and it works on every device regardless of flash hardware. Full-screen colour with WindowManager.LayoutParams.screenBrightness = 1.0f and the keep-screen-on flag; render through Compose with a withFrameNanos loop so ramps land on real vsync boundaries.

13.3The scheduler

One dedicated thread at THREAD_PRIORITY_URGENT_AUDIO owns all output. It holds the frozen cue array and a monotonic clock, and does nothing else.

  • SCH-01
    Monotonic clock only. SystemClock.elapsedRealtimeNanos(). Wall clock jumps on NTP correction and timezone change; using it means a show can skip mid-song.
  • SCH-02
    No Handler.postDelayed, no coroutine delay. Both are subject to message-queue backlog, Doze and GC pauses, and both routinely overshoot by tens of milliseconds. Sleep in a loop to within 2 ms of target, then busy-wait the remainder.
  • SCH-03
    Never allocate in the loop. No boxing, no iterators, no logging on the hot path. A GC pause during a chorus is a visible glitch.
  • SCH-04
    Drop, don't queue. A cue whose time has already passed by more than 30 ms is discarded, never fired late. Late light is worse than absent light — the eye reads it as wrong, not as delayed.
  • SCH-05
    Foreground service with a wake lock so the show survives the screen timing out or the user switching apps mid-track.

13.4Playback clock in Phase 1

The single most common way to get this wrong

In Phase 1 the app is both the audio source and the light source, so it is tempting to start a timer when playback starts and schedule from that. Don't — AudioTrack buffers 50–200 ms and the exact figure varies by device, route and whether Bluetooth is involved. A Bluetooth speaker adds 150–250 ms on top of that.

Use AudioTrack.getTimestamp(), which returns a (frame position, nanoTime) pair describing which frame is actually leaving the DAC. Sample it every ~200 ms, fit a line, and drive the scheduler from that fit. When it is unavailable, fall back to getPlaybackHeadPosition() minus AudioManager.getProperty(PROPERTY_OUTPUT_FRAMES_PER_BUFFER) and mark the session as low-confidence in telemetry.

15Safety & compliance

A flashing light in a dark arena in front of a large crowd. This section outranks every other requirement in this document.

Non-negotiable

Photosensitive epilepsy affects roughly 1 in 4,000 people, and seizures are provoked most readily between 3 and 30 Hz, with peak sensitivity around 15–20 Hz. WCAG 2.3.1 permits no more than three general flashes per second. Ofcom's guidance for public events recommends staying under 4 Hz. A torch LED is a small, extremely high-contrast point source in a dark room — the worst case for this risk, and there will be thousands of them.

At an arena's scale, "1 in 4,000" means a dozen susceptible people in a 50,000-seat bowl, every single night. This is not a theoretical compliance box.

15.2The limiter, and the BPM arithmetic

3 Hz is exactly 180 BPM. Flashing on every beat is therefore safe for most music — 128 BPM house is 2.13 Hz, 140 BPM trap is 2.33 Hz — and unsafe above 180: drum & bass at 174 is borderline, hardstyle at 150 with eighth-note hits is 5 Hz and plainly over. The limiter is a real code path that fires often, not a formality.

// runs on every compile; may delete cues; cannot be disabled
1. sort cues by time
2. sliding 1 s window over the cue list
3. while window.count > 3:
     drop the lowest-salience cue in the window   // keep the musical hits
4. if drops exceeded 20% of cues:
     escalate subdivision (beats → 2nd → 4th → downbeats), recompile
     tell the user: "Auto-set to every 2nd beat — 174 BPM exceeds
                     the safe flash rate on every beat."
5. assert max rate ≤ 3.0 Hz or refuse to arm the show

Note step 3's ordering: when we must drop cues we drop the weakest ones, so the surviving pattern still lands on the hits that matter. A naive every-other-cue decimation would produce a rhythmically wrong show that is merely safe.

15.3User-facing safety

  • SAF-01
    Consent before the first show, not at install. A plain-language photosensitivity warning naming the risk, with equal-weight Continue and Use no-flash mode buttons. Recorded with a timestamp and re-shown after any update that changes the limiter.
  • SAF-02
    Reduced-flash mode. Screen-only, smooth ramps, no transition faster than 1 Hz. Reachable in one tap from the show screen, and honoured as a persistent preference.
  • SAF-03
    Respect ACCESSIBILITY_DISPLAY_ANIMATION_DISABLED. If the user has told the OS they don't want animation, default them into reduced-flash and say why.
  • SAF-04
    Panic stop. Any touch anywhere on the show screen stops all output within 100 ms. Also bound to either volume key. No confirmation dialog.
  • SAF-05
    Three-second countdown before any show arms, with a visible cancel. Nobody's phone starts strobing unexpectedly in their pocket.
  • SAF-06
    Never fire when face-down or pocketed. Proximity plus accelerometer; a torch strobing against fabric is a burn and battery risk with no upside.
  • SAF-07
    Ship the limiter's report. The show detail screen states the peak flash rate of the compiled show in Hz. Designers should be able to see the number, not trust a promise.

15.4Thermal & power

Subscribe to PowerManager.addThermalStatusListener and degrade in defined stages rather than letting the OS throttle us unpredictably: at MODERATE drop torch strength 30%; at SEVERE switch to screen-only and tell the user; at CRITICAL stop the show. Mirror this for battery: below 15% offer to switch to screen-only, below 5% stop. A phone that dies at the encore is a worse outcome than a dimmer show.

17.1Microphone commitments

  • Captured audio lives in a ring buffer and is never written to disk and never transmitted.
  • Only spectral peak hashes are derived from it, and those are discarded within seconds. They are not reversible to audio, and we should still not keep them.
  • Capture stops the instant the show stops or the user leaves the listen screen. The mic indicator in the status bar is a feature, not a problem — it tells the truth.
  • State this in the rationale screen in one sentence, not in a privacy policy nobody reads.

17.2Content rights

Phase 1 and 2 analyse files the user already possesses, on their own device, and never transmit or reproduce them. That is the same posture as any local music player and is well-trodden ground. Two things to keep clear of: do not upload user audio to any analysis service (which is a reason §6 avoids the cloud option), and do not distribute beat maps for commercial recordings as a content library without legal review — a beat map is arguably factual metadata, but "arguably" is not a position to build a catalogue on. Phase 3 show packages for a client's own tracks are covered by that client's contract.

22Test strategy

22.1Analysis correctness

The DSP core is pure C++ with no Android dependency, so it is tested on the host in CI against a fixture corpus of 60 tracks with hand-annotated beats spanning rock, electronic, hip-hop, jazz, classical, Bollywood, Afrobeats and metal. Metrics are standard MIR: F-measure at ±70 ms, plus continuity-based scores. Any commit that moves F₁ more than 0.02 in either direction fails the build and requires a human to look at it — including improvements, because a jump usually means a fixture broke.

22.2Safety limiter

Property-based tests: for any generated cue list, at any tempo from 40 to 300 BPM, with any style and any edit set, the limiter's output must never exceed 3.0 Hz in any 1 s window. Thousands of generated cases per run. This is the one test suite that gates release unconditionally.

22.3Timing — the photodiode rig

You cannot measure light output from inside the phone, and self-reported timing is exactly the thing that will be wrong. Build the rig:

  • A photodiode taped over the torch LED, into an oscilloscope or a cheap USB DAQ.
  • The phone's line-out (or a microphone on its speaker) into the second channel.
  • Play a test track with known beat positions; capture both channels; measure the distribution of light-onset minus audio-beat.
  • Report mean offset (which becomes torchLatencyMs) and standard deviation (which is the jitter figure in §14).

Run this for every device in the matrix, every release. A 240 fps phone camera pointed at both devices is an acceptable low-budget substitute at ~4 ms resolution, which is enough to catch anything that matters.

23.1On the install problem specifically

It is worth being blunt about this because it is the risk most likely to make a technically excellent product commercially irrelevant. CUE started with a native app and moved to a QR-code web page, and that was almost certainly the most valuable decision they made. The browser can drive the torch (ImageCapture with the torch constraint) and the screen, and it can decode audio from getUserMedia — so a web client is technically feasible for the fan tier, with meaningfully worse timing and no background execution.

The resolution is probably that these are two products sharing a beat-map format: a native app for the people who design and rehearse shows, and a web client for the fifty thousand people who just scanned a code. That is a Phase 3 decision, but the format work in §7 should be done with it in mind — which is why FMT-03 requires portability now rather than later.

Vocal handlingHPSS rather than neural stem separation. No model file, seconds not minutes, and the accuracy difference does not justify 80 MB.
Output channelsTorch, screen, haptics and an on-screen visualiser, all driven from one compiled cue list.
PlatformNative Android, Kotlin + Compose, with a C++ DSP core kept portable for an eventual iOS port.

Still open

  1. Who is the first paying customer — an artist or a team? It changes almost nothing in Phase 1 and almost everything in Phase 3. A touring artist needs portable show packages and works with live performance, which pushes ultrasonic up the roadmap. A sports team has one fixed venue, a fixed PA and a game-ops budget, which makes a venue install far easier to sell.
  2. Do we commit to a web fan client? (§23.1) If yes, the beat-map format and the show compiler should be designed to run in a browser from the start, and that is a real constraint on M1–M3 rather than a later port.
  3. Should Phase 1 ship publicly, or stay internal? A standalone "make your phone flash to your music" app is a genuinely fun consumer product that would generate device profiles and beat-map corpus data for free. It also shows the whole idea to competitors a year early.
  4. How much of a show does the designer author versus the algorithm? This document assumes auto-generate-then-nudge. A full timeline editor is a much bigger product and a different company.
  5. Does the torch-only path really avoid the camera foreground-service type? (§16) Worth verifying on Samsung and Xiaomi specifically in M0, because if it does not, the permission story in §17 gets meaningfully worse and onboarding needs rework.
